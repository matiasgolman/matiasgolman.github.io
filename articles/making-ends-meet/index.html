Not making ends meet: what Argentine households do when income falls short

In October, Argentina's President asked how someone who runs out of money on the 20th could go ten days without eating. Official survey data tell a different story: not making ends meet means spending savings, borrowing, looking for more hours of work without finding them or dropping private health cover. And the middle class is hit hardest.

The column (English translation)

Translated by the author from the Spanish original published in El Economista on 13 November 2026.

On 19 October, in Neuquén, the President asked how someone who runs out of money on the 20th could live ten days without eating or drinking. The question takes the expression literally. It seems childish to have to spell it out, but "not making it to the end of the month" does not describe someone who stops eating. It describes a household whose income does not cover its usual spending and which closes that gap with adjustments it had not planned for. Making it to the end of the month is a subjective notion for each family. For some it means not being able to save for the holidays. For others, buying lower-quality products or cutting the number of meals a day. The President looks at reality through a literal reading of economic theory, in which, if the price of a product goes up and you substitute that consumption for something else, it is a rational decision and not an adjustment. But not even the most classical theory sees it that way: whoever substitutes because something became more expensive ends up worse off. There lies the semantic myopia. The fallacy of literalism.

Now, the government maintained and increased transfers to the poorest households. That is why, when one looks at the macro numbers, this change in consumption (popularly known as… austerity) does not show up in poverty. According to INDEC, the national statistics office, poverty fell from 41.7% of people in the second half of 2023 to 28.2% in the second half of 2025, and rose again to 32.3% in the first half of 2026. Structurally, there are no more poor people than before. The adjustment lies elsewhere, and a good part of it does show up in INDEC's Permanent Household Survey (EPH). This column looks at it channel by channel, using the microdata from 2011 to the second quarter of 2026.

Economic theory rests on simplifying people's behaviour in order to draw conclusions. Let us make some simplifications of our own. A household whose income falls short has, broadly speaking, four mechanisms at hand: borrowing and dissaving (spending savings, borrowing, selling things), working more (more hours, another job, another member who goes out to look for work), receiving help (from the State or from relatives) and consuming less or worse. To see who uses each one, we split the population into five groups of equal size according to the per capita income of their household (quintiles). The first is the poorest 20%; the second, the lower middle class; the third, the middle class; the fourth, the upper middle class; and the fifth, the richest 20%.

Borrowing and dissaving, the mechanism that moved the most

The EPH asks whether, in the last three months, the household lived, among other things, off its savings or off loans. In the first quarter of 2024, 38.4% of households said they had spent savings, the highest value since 2011 (the previous peak, 36.7%, came in the middle of the pandemic). It happened equally across all quintiles. But it was a jump, not a trend. In 2025 it fell to 34.9%, and in the second quarter of 2026 it stood at 34.0%, barely above the levels of 2019 and 2023 (32.3% and 32.0%). What did change persistently was credit. Loans from banks and finance companies went from 10.1% of households in 2023 to 14.0% in 2025, and at the end of 2025 they hit a record (14.9%, when until 2024 the ceiling had been 14.0%). The survey question refers to loans from "banks, finance companies, etc.", so it probably also captures credit from digital wallets and other online platforms, which expanded a great deal in these years. According to the Argentine Fintech Chamber, in early 2025 some 5.2 million people had a loan from these platforms, 65% more than a year earlier.

Who borrows from banks and finance companies? A bit of everyone, because credit grew in all five quintiles. But the highest level is in the middle. In 2025, 16.9% of middle-class households did so, and 16.2% of lower-middle-class households, against 13.1% among the richest 20%; in the second quarter of 2026, the figure for the lower middle class had already reached 17.3%. The poorest finance themselves in other ways, with loans from relatives or friends (27.2% of first-quintile households in 2025, against 8.6% in the fifth) and by selling belongings (15.7%). Households whose main earner is a woman, or someone who did not finish secondary school, rely more on loans from relatives and friends (16.7% and 18.8%, against 14.7% and 10.5% when the main earner is a man or someone with a tertiary degree).

Working more, when there are no more hours

On the labour side, the adjustment of these years is less visible. It is true that more people hold more than one job today than a decade ago. In 2024 and 2025, 11.5% of the employed had more than one, when until 2017 the share had never exceeded 9%. But that rise predates 2024: it happened mostly between 2017 and 2019 and in the exit from the pandemic. Since 2023 the total has barely changed (10.8% in 2023), and the chart shows this in every quintile. It rose somewhat only in the upper middle class (10.7% to 12.1%) and fell in the two poorest quintiles (in the first, from 8.2% to 7.7%). Multiple jobholding is more common among women (14.7%, against 8.9% of men) and in the richest quintile (16.0%), where teaching, health and the professions abound.

Hours did not rise in any group either. Average hours across all jobs were 38.5 a week in 2023 and 38.1 in 2025; in the poorest quintile, 34.2 and 34.1. Those with a second job are not working more hours either (14.2 and 14.2 hours in the second job), and the share working more than 45 hours fell (30.0% to 28.9%). What grew is the number of people who want to work more and cannot, which went from 17.3% to 19.0% of the employed (in the poorest quintile, from 30.3% to 34.3%). There are more people looking for hours than hours available. And the employment that is growing is informal. According to the Interdisciplinary Institute of Political Economy at the University of Buenos Aires (IIEP), in June 2026 there were 246,000 fewer formal private-sector wage jobs than in November 2023, and informality reached 45% of the employed in the second quarter of 2026, 1.8 points more than a year earlier, almost all of it due to the rise in self-employment (IIEP).

Does another household member go out to work when the main earner's income falls? The EPH re-interviews the same households three months and one year later, which makes it possible to look. Among members who were neither working nor looking for work, 16.7% started doing so in the following quarter when the main earner's real income had fallen by more than 10%; 14.2% when it had held steady; and 17.3% when it had risen.1 When the main earner lost their job, the share was lower (13.4%). In the description, then, no clear "added worker" appears. Households whose incomes move, up or down, are also the ones that enter and leave the labour market most. And the fact that fewer members enter when the main earner loses their job may be because the blow hits the whole household at once (a family business that closes, a neighbourhood with no work). Separating cause from correlation is left for future research.

Receiving help, or how the AUH holds up those at the bottom

In the poorest quintile, about half of households receive a State subsidy or benefit (45.2% in 2023, 44.6% in 2025); the Universal Child Allowance (AUH), which in the survey includes the Food Benefit (the former Food Card), reaches 42.0%. Further up, these transfers barely exist. Coverage did not change, but the amount did. The average transfer received by each first-quintile household, measured in today's pesos, was 24.1% higher in 2025 than in 2023, and went from 14.5% to 16.1% of its total income. Since December 2023 the AUH has risen faster than inflation, and in early 2024 so did the Food Benefit, which doubled and is an important part of what families receiving the AUH collect. Taken together, in May 2026 they were worth 52% more in real terms than in November 2023 (Chequeado). The Food Benefit, however, was frozen for almost two years from June 2024 and over that period lost 46% of its real value (ODSA-UCA). The other benefits were frozen too. The rise in these transfers accounts for about 3.5 points of the improvement in the poorest quintile's real income.

Help that does not come from the State also grew. Receiving goods in kind (from the State, churches or schools) became common during the pandemic and never went back, at 7.3% of the poorest households in 2019 and 19.7% in 2025. Help from relatives or neighbours who do not live in the household rose from 6.9% to 8.3% of all households.

Consuming less or worse

The EPH does not measure consumption. We do not know whether a household switched brands of yerba mate or stopped going to the cinema. Other sources do look at it, and they point in the same direction. According to the consultancy Scentia, mass consumption fell 2.9% in the first half of 2026 and is 17.1% below January 2023. Worldpanel by Numerator records households buying less often, in smaller volumes and more budget brands, and beef consumption, according to the industry chamber (CICCRA), stands at 47 kilos per person a year, the lowest level in two decades. E-commerce, which is growing fast (41% year on year in June), is not enough to make up for it, because according to the Argentine E-commerce Chamber it accounts for no more than 10% of sales in most companies.

What the EPH does ask about are two consumption items that matter: health coverage and school. In health, the share of people with neither social insurance (obra social) nor private health cover went from 31.7% in 2023 to 32.7% in 2025, and in the second quarter of 2026 it reached 34.2%. The rise was not at the bottom, where they were already the majority, but in the middle. In the middle class it went from 18.3% to 20.2% (and to 24.6% in the second quarter of 2026), and in the upper middle class from 9.0% to 11.1%. In the lower middle class, private cover shrank: it covered 4.9% of people in 2023 and 2.9% in 2025. That is more people who come to depend on the public hospital.

That consumption worsens while poverty falls is not a contradiction. Official poverty compares each household's income with the total basic basket, which is made up mostly of food, precisely what rose the least. Between November 2023 and June 2026 that basket rose 292%, against 320% for the CPI and 332% for the middle-class basket. With disinflation and the rise in the AUH, many households ended up above that line even though their income does not stretch to pay for private health cover, rent or school fees, which rose much more. The line measures whether a household can buy the basics, not whether it can keep up what it used to consume. On top of that, since 2024 the survey records some incomes better, above all pensions, and that alone lowers measured poverty.

At school the signal is weaker. Of the pupils who were in a private school, 20.4% were in a public one a year later in 2025, against an average of 18.1% between 2022 and 2024. It is not a record (in 2021 it was 23.7%), and the difference is at the edge of the survey's margin of error, with no differences between girls and boys. School dropout among 13- to 16-year-olds, which the literature on Argentine crises links to the household head losing their job (Ciaschi and Neidhöfer, 2024), shows no increase for now (3.2% in 2025, against 2.8% between 2022 and 2024).2

Prices did not rise equally for everyone

Not all households buy the same things, and the average hides it. INDEC's CPI still uses the 2004/05 basket as weights. If the spending structure of each quintile from the 2017/18 National Household Expenditure Survey (ENGHo) is used instead, between November 2023 and June 2026 the basket of the poorest 20% rose 322%, that of the middle class 332% and that of the richest 20% 337%, against 320% for the official CPI. The reason is composition. Food, which is 34.5% of spending in the poorest quintile and 15.7% in the richest, rose 293%, well below housing and utilities (602%), education (443%), communications (427%) or transport (383%).

Why does it matter? Because wage agreements and pensions are adjusted, when they are adjusted, by the average. If what you consume rose more than the average, a raise equal to official inflation does not give you back your purchasing power. Over these two and a half years, the baskets of all quintiles rose more than the official CPI, and the more so the higher the income. From the middle class upwards, the gap is already more than ten points.

Who is not making it

If each quintile's income is deflated by its own quintile's basket, real per capita income in 2025 was 13.8% higher than in 2023 in the poorest quintile, 10.9% in the richest and between 9.7% and 9.9% in the three middle ones. In the latest data the difference is starker. In the second quarter of 2026, compared with the same quarter of 2023, the first quintile was 6.1% higher and the fifth 7.2%, but the lower middle class only 2.6%, the middle class 1.1% and the upper middle class 1.1%.

But 2023 was a year of very high inflation and is too low a benchmark. The comparison that matters most is with 2017, the last year with inflation of the current order before the 2018 crisis. Compared with 2017, real income in 2025 is still 14.6% lower in the lower middle class and 14.0% in the middle class, and also 9.7% lower among the poorest 20% and 9.3% among the richest. No group has returned to that level, and most are barely where they were in 2019, before the pandemic and the inflationary spiral that followed. That memory, of having been able to buy more a few years ago, is the yardstick each family uses to judge whether it is making it or not. And perhaps it is easier to use now. With high inflation, prices stop being a good guide, and comparing what used to be enough with what is enough today becomes almost impossible (Heymann and Leijonhufvud, 1995). With lower inflation, the comparison becomes possible again. It is the B-side of disinflation: purchasing power improves, but so does the ability to realise how much was lost.

That real income rose in every quintile compared with 2023 also has a measurement explanation. Since late 2023 INDEC has asked separately about pensions, their bonuses and back pay, and about each type of transfer, and since 2024 the EPH has captured these non-labour incomes better. According to Equilibra, the pensions reported in the survey went from the equivalent of 85% of what ANSES, the social security agency, pays out to exceeding it (110%). For labour incomes the debate is more open. The wage of registered employees reported in the survey rose 42.7% in the fourth quarter of 2025, against 32.0% in administrative records (SIPA) and inflation of 31.5%, but the questionnaire change did not target those incomes and there is no agreement on the cause of the gap. Several economists estimate that a significant part of the fall in poverty is due to the improvement in measurement.3

The government argues that informal workers are driving the recovery. In the EPH, the real income of unregistered employees did indeed rise 24.3% between 2023 and 2025 and that of the self-employed 29.4%, against 12.7% for registered employees. There are three caveats. The first is measurement. The rise the survey records for non-employees (70% year on year at the end of 2025, according to Equilibra) is hard to reconcile with other indicators, and the official index of unregistered wages comes from the same EPH with a lag of about six months, which distorts comparisons between periods of high and low inflation (Tiempo Argentino). The second is the starting point, because compared with 2017 informal workers are still lower too (6.7% for unregistered employees). The third is the level. An unregistered employee earns on average 48% of what a registered one earns. Even comparing workers with the same education, age, sector and firm size, the IIEP estimates that an informal worker earned 34% less than a formal one in early 2026. The gap has narrowed (39% a year earlier), but it is still large. And unregistered workers are still more than a third of all employees (36.7% in 2025). All in all, what can be stated with confidence is the comparison between income groups, in which the middle class is the one that has recovered least.

Four mechanisms: so where does that leave us?

Set side by side, the four mechanisms tell a coherent story. Two forces outside the household's control push in opposite directions. State help protects those at the bottom, because the AUH rose faster than inflation and State transfers are now a sixth of the poorest quintile's income. Prices, by contrast, punish more the higher up one is, because services, education and transport, which rose the most, weigh more in those baskets. The poorest 20% have the help and the richest 20% a cushion of income and savings. The middle class has neither. It receives no transfers and its basket became almost as expensive as that of the rich, which is why its real income is the one that has recovered least.

That is why the mechanisms that depend on the household itself are concentrated in the middle. It is the middle class that borrows most from banks and finance companies and that most often lost social insurance or private health cover. The poorest finance themselves through relatives and by selling belongings, and want to work more hours that they cannot find. Work is stuck for everyone, with hours flat and more people looking for hours that are not there. None of these pressures shows up in the poverty rate, which measures the distance to a line and not the cost of staying above it.

What these data do not say

Everything above describes how each mechanism changed, not how much of that change is due to the fall in income; these are not causal effects. The EPH covers 31 urban areas. The comparisons use annual averages, so as not to compare isolated quarters, and in the charts 2026 includes only the quarters already published.4 The charts mark with a dotted line the inauguration of the current government, in December 2023.5 The quintile basket uses 2017/18 weights without substitution, so it is an upper bound on the difference between baskets.

Nobody lives ten days without eating. What millions of households do is something else. They spend what they had saved, they borrow, they look for more hours of work, they drop their private health cover, they move the children to public school and they buy cheaper. The relevant question is not whether people eat on the 25th, but how much it costs them to get there, and who pays that cost.

If Paul McCartney comes back to Argentina, perhaps the President could ask him why Lady Madonna did not die on the 20th. Lady Madonna, children at your feet. Wonder how you manage to make ends meet. Paul does not ask her whether she has eaten. He knows people find a way. What intrigues him is how she does it.

Summary

This report accompanies the Spanish-language column «No llegar a fin de mes» (“Not making it to the end of the month”). Using microdata from INDEC’s Permanent Household Survey (EPH) between 2011 and the second quarter of 2026, it describes four ways out for a household whose current income does not cover its usual spending: borrowing and dissaving, working more, receiving help, and consuming less or worse. It adds the change in relative prices across income groups, measured with a quintile-specific price index, and an approximation to the effect of state transfers on poverty. All results are shown by quintile of per capita household income and, where useful, by gender and education.

There are six main findings. (i) Borrowing and dissaving moved the most. Spending savings jumped in the first quarter of 2024 (38.4% of households) but the jump did not last, while loans from banks and finance companies, which probably include credit from digital wallets, rose from 10.1% in 2023 to 14.0% in 2025, with the highest levels in the middle class (second and third quintiles). (ii) The labour adjustment does not show in hours, which did not rise in any quintile, nor in multiple jobholding, which is higher than before 2017 but has barely changed since 2023 (10.8% in 2023 and 11.5% in 2025). (iii) The EPH panels show no clean added-worker effect in the descriptive evidence. (iv) The coverage of state transfers in the poorest quintile did not change, but their real value rose: without them, approximate poverty in the second quarter of 2026 would have been 2.4 points higher, against 1.5 points in 2023. (v) The share of people without contributory or private health coverage increased, mostly in the middle and upper-middle classes; moves from private to public school rose in 2025 at the edge of the margin of error, and teenage dropout shows no change. (vi) Since November 2023 the basket of richer households has become more expensive than that of the poorest; with that index, the real income of the middle class recovered less than at both extremes, although the better income capture of the EPH since 2024 probably overstates the rise for every group. Everything is descriptive; causal identification is left to the work that follows (last section).

Question and framework

“Not making it to the end of the month” describes a gap: the household’s usual spending exceeds its current income. The gap is closed somehow, because the household keeps consuming. The empirical question is through which channel and for whom. There are four ways out:

  1. Borrowing and dissaving: spending savings, borrowing from relatives, banks or finance companies, buying in instalments or on credit, selling belongings.
  2. Working more: more hours in the main job, a second job, or other members entering the labour market (the “added worker”; Lundberg, 1985; Stephens, 2002). For Argentina, Paz (2009) estimates this effect with the EPH 2003–2007, and Ciaschi and Neidhöfer (2024) find with the EPH 1995–2015 that the male head’s job loss raises the spouse’s labour force participation and the children’s school dropout.
  3. Receiving help: state transfers, food or goods, help from relatives who live elsewhere.
  4. Consuming less or worse: cheaper brands, public instead of private school, public hospitals instead of private health plans, less leisure (McKenzie and Schargrodsky, 2011, for the 2002 crisis).

Relative prices are not a way out but a condition: if what the household buys rises faster than the general index, the gap widens even if nominal income keeps up with official inflation (Kaplan and Schulhofer-Wohl, 2017; Jaravel, 2021). In a country where wages and pensions are adjusted by the average, that difference becomes a loss of purchasing power for the groups whose basket rises more.

A fall in the real wage has income and substitution effects of opposite sign on labour supply; with a generalised fall only the net effect is observed. Supply rising near the subsistence floor is what one expects if the income effect dominates (Dessing, 2002). Separating both effects requires wage variation independent of non-labour income, which is not pursued here.

Data and definitions

Microdata sources. We use the EPH files cleaned and harmonised by the Encuestas LATAM project, built with our own Stata code from INDEC’s original microdata (59 quarters from 2011Q1, 3.13 million individual records). The cleaning applies, among other things, the 2016Q2–2018Q4 weight splice that INDEC published in the methodological note of the first quarter of 2019 (11 urban areas; here it is also applied to the household file), handles non-response in main-job income, and harmonises codes that INDEC changes across quarters. With INDEC’s raw microdata the figures in this report change by less than a tenth of a point. The household file (strategies V1–V19) is the one harmonised by Encuestas LATAM. INDEC did not publish 2007Q3 or 2015Q3–2016Q1. This version includes the second quarter of 2026, the latest released by INDEC at the time of writing (5 November 2026).

Quintiles. People are ranked by the per capita income of their household (EPH decile DECCFR, all urban areas) and grouped into five equal-sized groups. In the text, the first quintile is “the poorest 20%”; the second, the lower-middle class; the third, the middle class; the fourth, the upper-middle class; the fifth, “the richest 20%”. Households that do not report income are excluded from the quintile breakdowns, not from the total. Quintiles have twice as many observations as deciles (roughly 2,000 to 2,800 households per quarter since 2024, more in earlier years), so changes can be read with less noise.

Main earner. The household member with the highest total individual income; ties go to the household head. Their gender and education (less than secondary, secondary completed or some tertiary, tertiary completed) are used to describe households.

Household file. Questions V1 to V19: “in the last three months, have the members of this household lived on…?”. We use spending savings (V13), loans from relatives or friends (V14), loans from banks or finance companies (V15), instalment or credit purchases (V16), selling belongings (V17), a state cash benefit (V5; from the fourth quarter of 2023 split into the universal child allowance AUH, other programmes and aid from churches or NGOs, merged back for the series), food or goods (V6) and help from non-residents (V7). Totals use the PONDERA weight; quintiles use PONDIH, the weight that corrects for income non-response.

Individual file. Hours in all jobs, more than one job, wanting more hours, pension contributions of employees, health coverage and non-labour income amounts. State cash transfers are, up to the third quarter of 2023, the “subsidy or social aid” amount; from the fourth, the sum of AUH and the pregnancy allowance (which includes the Tarjeta Alimentar food card) and other government programmes.

Panels. The EPH interviews each dwelling for two consecutive quarters, drops it for two and interviews it for two more. This allows the same people to be followed one quarter and one year later. Records are matched by dwelling, household and member number, with the same gender and a compatible age. They are weighted with the first-visit weight, which is not longitudinal: levels are biased by attrition and response error; changes across years are what is read.

Prices. From December 2016, national CPI by division up to June 2026, weighted with the expenditure shares by quintile of the National Household Expenditure Survey (ENGHo) 2017/18. Each quarter’s income is deflated with the previous month’s prices. Before December 2016 there is no reliable official CPI by division, and a single index is used for all quintiles: from January 2011 to April 2016, the Cavallo and Bertolotto (2016) series, which splices INDEC’s Greater Buenos Aires CPI (to 2006), the San Luis CPI (2007) and PriceStats’ online price index (from December 2007); from April to December 2016, INDEC’s Greater Buenos Aires CPI (base April 2016). Cross-check: the spliced series gives 37.6% in 2014 and 27.9% in 2015, against 38.0% and 26.9% for the Buenos Aires City CPI (IPCBA); in 2016 it gives 34.4% against 41.0%, because PriceStats underestimates early 2016 (utility tariffs). Real income levels for 2011–2015 may therefore be overstated by up to about 5% relative to 2017 onwards; they are read as context. The official CPI used in the calculations matches INDEC’s series exactly (datos.gob.ar), and 2025 inflation comes out at the official 31.5%.

Approximate poverty. Total household income is compared with the total basic basket (CBT) of the household’s region per adult equivalent (average of the quarter’s three months) and INDEC’s equivalence scale. The regional CBT is INDEC’s from April 2016 to December 2025 (datos.gob.ar; the Greater Buenos Aires series matches INDEC’s file exactly); from January 2026, INDEC’s Greater Buenos Aires CBT times each region’s 2025 ratio to it (stable: the ratios move less than one point across years); before April 2016, the April 2016 CBT taken back with the spliced price index (there is no reliable official CBT for those years). INDEC measures by half-year; here quarters are averaged. The approximation tracks the official series closely: between 2016 and the first half of 2026 it is 0.3–0.9 points below (27.9% against 28.2% in the second half of 2025, and 31.7% against 32.3% in the first half of 2026, the first complete half-year of 2026). The no-transfer counterfactual is used only in this report.

Margin of error. For schooling, measured in the panel with smaller samples, approximate 95% intervals are reported with a design effect of 2 (the EPH is a cluster sample and siblings share households): standard error = √(p(1−p)/(n/2)). Differences across years are assessed with the corresponding z statistic. This is a conservative approximation; INDEC does not publish replicate weights.

Periods. Comparisons use annual averages of the published quarters (2015 has two and 2016 three; INDEC did not publish 2015Q3–2016Q1) and the latest quarter separately. Figures mark December 2023, when the current government took office. Figures for 2011–2015 belong to the period for which INDEC itself advises caution; they are shown as context.

Seasonality of the latest year. 2026 only has the first and second quarters, and the figures compare it with full years. To rule out that the 2026* point reflects the calendar rather than a trend, every comparison was redone using first halves only (84 combinations of series and group, total and quintiles). Borrowing and dissaving

The share of households that lived on their savings went from about a third in 2019 and 2023 to a peak of 38.4% in the first quarter of 2024, the highest since 2003 (the previous peak, 36.7%, was in the second quarter of 2020). Other consumption. The EPH does not ask about food, brands, services or leisure. Income capture. Since the fourth quarter of 2023 the questionnaire asks separately about pensions, their bonuses and back payments, and each type of transfer (V5, V21 and V22 split into sub-items). The improvement in capture is concentrated in these non-labour incomes: according to Equilibra (2025), pensions reported in the EPH went from 85% of ANSES payments to 110%, while registered private wages went from 76% to 88% of SIPA, the smallest improvement among the sources compared. For labour income, which the questionnaire change did not target, the evidence is more contested. In the fourth quarter of 2025, the wage of registered employees in the EPH rose 42.7% year on year, against 32.0% in SIPA, 37.0% in RIPTE, 30.0% in INDEC’s wage index and inflation of 31.5% (Equilibra, as reported by Infobae). CEDLAS estimates that, correcting for under-reporting by source, the timing mismatch between incomes and baskets, and the Engel coefficient from ENGHo 2017/18, poverty would have fallen by about 2.0 points between the second half of 2023 and the first half of 2025, not ten. The most cited explanation is that under very high inflation respondents under-report their income more, and that disinflation reversed this bias. For this report, the consequence is that real income levels for 2024–2026 are probably overstated relative to 2023 in every quintile. Since the improvement is concentrated in pensions and transfers, which weigh differently in each quintile, the bias need not be even; Equilibra argues that it reaches every group, but there is no published evidence by quintile to tell whether it changes the ranking between groups, which is the result that matters here.

Informality. The government attributes the real wage recovery to informal workers. In the EPH, real income from the main job (P21, deflated by the official CPI) rose between 2023 and 2025 by 24.3% among unregistered employees, 29.4% among the self-employed and 12.7% among registered employees; against 2017, all three groups are still below (6.7%, 7.9% and 12.9%). The levels match those published by INDEC for the first quarter of 2026 (mean income of $727,000 and $1,372,000 for unregistered and registered employees, against INDEC’s $731,000 and $1,375,000; unregistered share of employees: 37.9%, against 37.9%). INDEC’s unregistered wage index, which shows a real rise of 84.0% since November 2023 according to Tiempo Argentino, faces three criticisms: it is built from the same EPH and reflects incomes about 6 months earlier, so it compares periods with different inflation (Pastrana, C-P); it inherits the survey’s improved income capture, which for non-employees shows 70.0% year on year at the end of 2025 (Equilibra); and it is inconsistent with other employment and consumption indicators (Maito, CETyD-UNSAM; Campos, CTA Autónoma). On the administrative side, formal private wage employment fell by 246,000 jobs between November 2023 and June 2026, almost 4% (IIEP-EDIL, 2026b), while the informality rate among the employed rose to 45% in the second quarter of 2026, 1.8 points higher than a year earlier, with own-account workers accounting for 91% of the increase (IIEP-EDIL, 2026a). The same report puts the informality wage penalty, comparing workers with the same characteristics, at 34% in the first quarter of 2026, against 39% a year earlier. The robust part is that informal income recovered more than formal income relative to 2023, from a very low floor, is still about half of it (48% of registered pay in 2025) and has not returned to its 2017 level.

Why 2017. 2023 is a low base (triple-digit inflation and income under-reporting). 2017 is the last year with inflation of the current order before the 2018 crisis, and it is the purchasing-power reference families have. Under high inflation, prices lose informational content and real comparisons become hard (Heymann and Leijonhufvud, 1995); disinflation makes them possible again. This is a hypothesis consistent with the ‘nominal memory’ Equilibra uses to explain 2023 under-reporting, not a result of this report.

Limits of the quintile index. ENGHo quintiles are of households and EPH quintiles of people: the match is approximate. The index uses a fixed basket, with no substitution, so it is an upper bound on the difference between groups. The published ENGHo does not cross quintile with region, and region matters because utility tariffs rose more in the Buenos Aires metropolitan area.

Synthesis: the four channels

The channels do not all push in the same direction, and that is why the pressure concentrates on the middle class.

  • Receiving help is progressive: state transfers are 16.1% of first-quintile income and almost nothing above the second; their real value rose (24.1% per first-quintile recipient household, 2025 against 2023).
  • Relative prices go the other way: the basket became more expensive the higher the income (322% in the first quintile, 332% in the third and 337% in the fifth, against 320% for the official CPI).
  • Real income, deflated by each group’s own basket, combines both and is U-shaped: the extremes recovered more than the middle. It is not that the rich did best: the first quintile rose most, the fifth followed, and the second and third rose least.
  • Borrowing and dissaving: dissaving was even and short-lived (a jump in early 2024); credit from banks and finance companies, which probably includes digital-wallet credit, peaks in the second and third quintiles; at the bottom, help from relatives and selling belongings.
  • Working more: neither more hours nor, since 2023, more jobs in the total; multiple jobholding rose only in the fourth quintile; unmet demand for hours grows mostly at the bottom. No clear added-worker effect.
  • Consuming worse: the loss of health coverage concentrates in the third and fourth quintiles; schooling gives a weak signal.

The conclusion that the middle class bears the strongest pressure does not come from a single channel but from their combination: it does not receive the help that protects the bottom, it faces basket inflation almost as high as the top without the top’s cushion, and so its real income recovered least; and the strategies that depend on the household itself (bank credit and the loss of health coverage) concentrate there. None of this shows up in the poverty rate, which measures the distance to a line, not the cost of staying above it.

Next steps

  1. Added worker and dropout with Latin American panels since 2003. Extend the design of Ciaschi and Neidhöfer (2024), which ends in 2015 and uses only Argentina and male heads: every country with a panel, a main earner of either gender, their own labour supply and that of the other members, and household strategies as outcomes.
  2. Continuous erosion of the real wage. Continuous treatment: the change in the real wage of the main earner’s industry (registered employees’ pay by industry and province, deflated by the quintile index) between panel visits; difference-in-differences with a continuous treatment (Callaway, Goodman-Bacon and Sant’Anna, 2024) and threshold estimation (Hansen, 2000).
  3. Transfers and poverty. Reproduce the official half-yearly measurement and separate the contributions of AUH, other programmes and pensions; contrast EPH incomes with administrative records by quintile.
  4. Consumption. Match the EPH with the ENGHo as a compositional counterfactual.
