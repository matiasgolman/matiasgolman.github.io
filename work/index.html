Working papers

  • 2026

    The effect of AI adoption on the demand for personality traits

    Evidence from 11.7 million UK online job vacancies

    Abstract

    Using text retrieval methods on 11.7 million online job vacancies (2017–2022), I find that 72% of vacancies require at least one Big Five personality trait. Extraversion is the most common (44%), followed by Conscientiousness (34%) and Openness (30%). After accounting for occupational change, personality demands are mostly stable over time. Vacancies requiring AI skills are 7.2 percentage points more likely to demand Openness, and firms increase their demand for Openness by 3.6 percentage points across all subsequent hiring after they adopt AI for the first time — suggesting that as firms adopt AI they increasingly value creativity and adaptability, well beyond the AI roles themselves.

  • 2026

    The value of personality

    The wage penalty paradox in the UK

    Abstract

    Jobs that mention personality traits offer 4.6% lower wages on average, which raises a paradox: why advertise for traits that are penalised? Using 11.7 million UK job postings (2017–2022), I test four explanations — correlation with low-pay job characteristics, compensating differentials, linguistic intensity, and demographic targeting. Occupation fixed effects absorb the most, but significant penalties remain. Penalties are larger in high-skill occupations, smaller in female-dominated roles, and rise sharply with the intensity of the language used. Employers appear to use trait language for several purposes at once rather than one.

  • 2026

    The effectiveness of formalisation policies in Latin America

    A meta-analysis of 79 impact evaluations. with Verónica Escudero and Elva López-Mourelo (ILO)

    Abstract

    The first comprehensive meta-analysis of formalisation policies in Latin America: 79 impact evaluations, 527 estimates, interventions from 1990 to 2020, including Spanish-language studies and unpublished government reports that earlier reviews missed. Formalisation policies mostly work, and adverse effects are rare (5.1% of estimates) — but publication bias substantially reduces effect sizes once corrected. Active labour market policies, information campaigns and labour inspections raise formality; social protection programmes with activation components produce the largest earnings effects but barely move formality at all. Benefits strengthen over time, and multi-component interventions outperform single-component ones.

    Labour market informality in Costa Rica

    International Labour Organization

    A diagnostic of informal employment for the national formalisation framework: a review of a decade of studies and legal instruments, an analysis of how informal employment has moved across demographic and territorial lines using household survey microdata, and econometric models of its structural determinants. Recommendations aligned with ILO Recommendation 204 and the FORLAC 2.0 strategy.
